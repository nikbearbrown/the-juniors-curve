# Help with The Junior's Curve

*The Junior's Curve* asks how entry-level STEM work is changing. One strand tracks what employers ask of new entrants in job postings. A separate software pilot asks how coding agents affect what first-year developers do and learn. It uses the work-phase framework from [A New Shape for Work](https://github.com/nikbearbrown/a-new-shape-for-work).

**Where it stands:** this is early-stage research. There are methods documents and a source audit. There is no manuscript, no trend has been reported, the pilot is not preregistered and no participants have been recruited. The curve in `data/illustrative-curve.tsv` is hypothetical, not an observation.

**If you have an hour:** read [methods/entry-level-stem.md](methods/entry-level-stem.md) and [open an issue](https://github.com/nikbearbrown/the-juniors-curve/issues/new) naming one way the comparison between cohorts could mislead.

## Pick a way to help

| Contribution | Where to start | What to send |
|---|---|---|
| **Review the posting method** | [methods/entry-level-stem.md](methods/entry-level-stem.md) | How would you identify an entry-level posting? Say where the proposed cohort definition would include the wrong postings or miss the right ones, with an example title. |
| **Critique the pilot design** | [methods/phase-measurement.md](methods/phase-measurement.md) | A confound, a missing outcome, or a way to measure effort or learning more directly. Say what it would cost to collect. |
| **Audit a source** | [sources.md](sources.md) | Pick one source. Confirm what it measures, its population and years, and its access terms, with a link. |
| **Describe entry-level work you know** | The work phases in the methods documents | Name the field and role. Say which tasks a newcomer did before AI tools and which they do now. State when your example applies. Keep it general; see the rules below. |
| **Add related work** | [sources.md](sources.md) | A study of early-career work, apprenticeship or AI use by juniors, with the claim it supports or contradicts. |

## Rules for this repo

- **Keep the claims separate.** Job ads measure stated demand. They do not measure hiring, working time, productivity or learning. The software pilot does not stand in for all STEM work.
- **Protect people and employers.** Do not post private messages, participant records, an employer's source code, or third-party data whose terms you have not checked.
- **Hypothetical is labelled hypothetical.** Do not present the illustrative curve, or any expected result, as a finding.

## How to submit

For a discussion, [open an issue](https://github.com/nikbearbrown/the-juniors-curve/issues/new) with the file or section, your evidence, and a proposed next step. For an edit, fork, branch, and open a focused pull request. Say what you changed and how you checked it. If you use an AI-generated answer, verify its citations and claims before submitting it.

If you want to collaborate beyond a single contribution, open an issue describing what you'd like to take on, and read [Credit and what we expect from authors](#credit-and-what-we-expect-from-authors) first.

## Credit and what we expect from authors

The author list isn't decided yet. We follow [Yale's guidance on authorship](https://provost.yale.edu/policies/academic-integrity/guidance-authorship-scholarly-or-scientific-publications). Under it, an author has done **all four** of these:

1. **Contributed to a component of the work:** its conception, design, conduct, analysis, or interpretation of at least part of the results.
2. **Helped write the paper:** drafted part of it, or revised it for substance, not just copy edits.
3. **Approved the final version** before submission.
4. **Agreed to be accountable** for the work, including helping answer questions about its accuracy or integrity.

Some contributions are valuable but don't by themselves make someone an author: a single issue, one audited source, or an example from your own field. Everyone who contributes that way is thanked by name in the acknowledgments, unless they ask not to be. To be an author, take on a component, such as building the entry-level cohort for an occupation family, designing and running part of the pilot, or owning the posting analysis for one field, and then do the writing, approval and accountability steps too. An AI model can't be an author, since it can't approve the paper or answer for it.

If you want to be an author, we expect you to:

- **Read the whole paper,** not just your section. You're putting your name on all of it.
- **Think about it, then give feedback.** Does the method answer the question? Are the comparisons fair? Do the numbers in the text match the tables, figures and data?
- **Suggest changes when they matter.** Don't make a suggestion just to have made one. If you find nothing to change, say so; that's a useful answer. A real problem, raised clearly with a proposed fix, is the most valuable thing you can give.
- **Disclose AI assistance.** AI tools are fine for code and drafting, but say where you used them. You stay accountable for everything you contribute.
- **Keep a record of what you did:** what, when, and where it lives (issue, pull request, commit, figure). The paper's contribution statement is drawn from it and lists each author's [CRediT roles](https://credit.niso.org/).
- **Sign off only when you can stand behind it.** Before submission, every author confirms in writing that they have read the full paper, agree with it, and accept accountability for it. If something is wrong or unclear, say so before approval, not after publication.

Raise authorship early, and we'll revisit it as roles change. Author order follows contribution. If you think your work merits authorship and it isn't reflected, say so before the final draft. Sending an issue or pull request does not by itself settle authorship.
